Before you start
A sweep is one slow, controlled strum where each note pops individually and immediately mutes — strings don't ring together. Your pick travels in one direction. The left hand does the muting by releasing pressure after each note. Start extremely slow — 40bpm slow. Speed is a byproduct of clean mechanics, not the goal.

This is the foundation of every sweep. Three strings, same fret, one motion each way. The hammer-on at the top (fret 5 to 8) and pull-off back down is what turns it from a strum into a sweep — without it you'd just be barring.

The muting is the hard part. Each finger releases pressure immediately after the pick passes — almost like a rolling barre where each finger lifts as the next engages.

Death metal use: Play your chug riff, then sweep this shape as a punctuation before returning to the chug. One sweep in = texture. Sweeping constantly = loses impact.
Minor Minor Triad — 5 String (Core Shape) Intermediate ▼
Tab — A minor, position 1 (fret 5 root on A string)
e |--------5--------| ← root A (octave) B |------5---------| ← C (minor 3rd) G |----5-----------| ← E (5th) D |--7------------| ← C (minor 3rd) A |-7-------------| ← A (root) E |--------------| A₇ |--------------| ; Full shape down then up: A |-7-↓------------| D |--7-↓-----------| G |---5-↓----------| B |----5-↓---------| e |-----5h8p5-↑----| ← hammer/pull at top, reverse B |--------5-↑------| G |---------5-↑-----| D |----------7-↑----| A |-----------7-↑---|

This is the bread and butter shape. Root on the A string (fret 7 for A minor), sweep down through D G B e, hammer at the top, sweep back up. The shape is almost the same fret on every string — that's what makes it physically learnable.

To move this to any other key: find the root on the A string and rebuild from there. The relative fret distances stay identical. Same shape, different starting position.

Born of Osiris move: Play this shape, then slide the entire shape up 3 frets and repeat. Same fingering, instant key climb. That cascading ascent sound is literally just this shape moved up the neck repeatedly.
Diminished Diminished Arpeggio — The Evil Cascade Intermediate ▼
Tab — A diminished (A C Eb)
e |------5--------| B |----4---------| ← Eb (flat 5 / tritone) G |--5-----------| ← C (minor 3rd) D |-7------------| ← A (root) ; The secret: diminished shape repeats every 3 frets ; Play shape at fret 7, then again at fret 10, then 13 ; Same fingering, climbs the neck, all still "fits" D |-7h10h13---| ← root climbs, shape repeats G |-5--8--11---| B |-4--7--10---| e |-5--8--11---|

Diminished arpeggios are the Born of Osiris sound. The tritone (flat 5) inside the shape is what makes it feel unstable and cascading. And the magic property: the shape repeats identically every 3 frets, so you can sweep it once and then slide it up the neck for an instant climbing effect without changing fingering.

The tritone inside every diminished chord is your diabolus in musica — the same interval you already use in your riff. This is just that interval stacked into a sweepable shape.

Integration: Chug on string 7 → sweep diminished arpeggio ascending → land back on open A. That's a complete musical phrase. The sweep is the "breath" in the middle of the chug.
7-String 6-String Sweep with String 7 Root Launch Intermediate ▼
Tab — using string 7 as launchpad into sweep
; This is the 7-string advantage nobody talks about ; Hit the low root on str7, sweep up through the chord A₇ |-0--------------| ← open A root, launch note E |--7------------| ← A (root, octave up) A |---7-----------| ← A (root) D |----7----------| ← C (minor 3rd) G |-----7---------| ← E (5th) — wait, recalc B |------8--------| ← C (minor 3rd) e |-------5h8p5--| ← A root, hammer/pull, reverse ; Sweep direction: str7 pick → continue UP through all strings ; One continuous downstroke covers str7 through e ; Then hammer at top and reverse upstroke back down ; The low A on str7 adds WEIGHT before the sweep lifts off ; Sounds massive. Standard 6-string can't do this.

This is your 7-string exclusive move. You hit the open low A as a launchpad — that deep thud — then the sweep ascends through all 6 remaining strings. It sounds enormous because the lowest possible note in the tuning launches the arpeggio.

No 6-string player can do this. It's one of the few things where the 7th string isn't just an "extra chug string" — it becomes part of the melodic architecture.

Practical tip: The str7 note will still be ringing as you sweep upward — that low A rumbles underneath the arpeggio. On high gain this creates a wall of sound effect. Control it with your palm as you move off str7.
What an open string cascade is
Open strings ring freely while your fret hand moves. A cascade uses them as pivot points — you play a fretted note, pull off to the open string, and while that open string sustains, your hand shifts position. The overlap between the ringing open note and your next fretted note creates the twinkle. Your hand is only ever in one place — the open strings do the rest.

Cascade Basic Open Pull-Off Cascade (Midwest Emo Root) Beginner ▼
Tab — single string, pull to open
; Each fretted note pulls off to open string ; Open string rings while hand repositions e |-7p0---5p0---3p0---2p0--| A e G e E e D e ; That descending line (A G E D) is a melody ; The open e between each note is the "twinkle" ; Hand jumps between positions while open e rings

This is the midwest emo move you already know. Each pull-off to open gives your hand time to reposition without silence. The open string is buying you time while sounding musical.

In standard tuning this is on the high e. In Drop A your open strings are different — open e (string 6) and open A (string 7) are your most useful cascade pivots since A is your root key.

Cascade Cross-String Open Cascade (The Twinkle) Intermediate ▼
Tab — cascades across multiple strings
; Falls across strings using open notes as bridges ; While one string rings open, pick moves to next string e |-0h3h5---------0h3h5--| B |------3p0----------| G |--------2p0--------| D |----------2p0------| ; Read it as one flowing line: ; e: 0h3h5 → B: 3p0 → G: 2p0 → D: 2p0 → e: 0h3h5 ; Each open string overlaps with the next fretted note ; creating that sustained waterfall effect

This is where the twinkle actually comes from. Multiple open strings overlapping — while the B string open note is still ringing, you're already fretting on G. The notes blur together in a musical way because they're all in key.

The reason it sounds hard to play is the overlap makes it sound like more notes are happening than there are. You're only ever fretting one thing at a time.

In Drop A: Your open A string (str7) adds a low resonance underneath any cascade happening on the upper strings. If you let it ring while cascading on strings 1–3, you get a full chord texture from two independent things happening.

The contrast is the technique. An open string cascade is bright, melodic, and airy. A palm muted chug on string 7 is dark, dead, and heavy. Putting them back to back makes each one hit harder than it would alone.

This is the deathcore answer to the midwest emo twinkle — same mechanical idea, completely different emotional context because of what comes after it.

Your specific riff context: Take your current tritone chug riff. Before the power chord stab, insert a 2-beat open cascade on strings 1–3. Then let the power chord land. The cascade makes the stab feel like a punch instead of just a note.
7-String String 7 Open Root Cascade (Your Exclusive) Intermediate ▼
Tab — open A7 drones under upper string cascade
; Let str7 ring open while cascading above it ; Two independent voices simultaneously e |--5p0h3p0---------| B |--------3p0-----| G |------------2p0| D |---------------| A |---------------| E |---------------| A₇ |-0-----------0--| ← open A, let ring under everything ; Hit str7 open, let it drone ; Immediately cascade up on strings 1-3 ; Low A rumbles under the high twinkle ; Sounds like two guitarists ; Pluck str7 with thumb (hybrid picking) while ; fingers handle the upper cascade — advanced ; OR just hit str7 and quickly mute after 1 beat

This is genuinely something 6-string players cannot do. The open low A drones underneath while a melodic cascade happens on the high strings. It sounds like rhythm and lead guitar simultaneously from one instrument.

The easier version: pick str7 open, let it ring for one beat, then cascade on the high strings while the low A fades naturally. You don't need to sustain it perfectly — even the decay of the low A adds weight.

Hybrid picking path: If you eventually learn hybrid picking (pick + middle/ring fingers), you can hold the low A drone with your pick while fingers cascade above. That's a longer term goal but worth knowing it exists.
The core principle
Sweeps and cascades aren't songs — they're punctuation. One sweep inside a chug riff hits harder than constant sweeping. A cascade before a breakdown drop creates contrast that makes the drop feel heavier. Use them sparingly and intentionally.

Bar 1–2 is your existing riff. You already have this. Bar 3 is new — an open cascade that lifts the texture and gives the listener a moment of melodic air before the weight returns. Bar 4 is the sweep punctuating the return to the chug.

The whole 4-bar phrase tells a micro-story: establish → contrast → punctuate → return. That's a complete musical idea. String four of these together with variation and you have a full song section.

Learn it in pieces: Don't try the full 4 bars immediately. Get bar 1-2 clean first (you already have it). Add bar 3 cascade next week. Add the bar 4 sweep the week after. By the time you chain them together each piece will already be in muscle memory.
